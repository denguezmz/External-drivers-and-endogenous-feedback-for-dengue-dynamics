A modelling framework integrating external drivers and endogenous feedback for long-term dengue dynamics

Dengue transmission is influenced by climate, vector ecology and imported infections, but whether these external drivers sufficiently explain interannual epidemic variation remains unclear. 
We coupled a climate-driven mosquito population model with an extended Ross–Macdonald transmission model and compared four formulations: an empirical temperature response, a neural network temperature response, endogenous feedback acting through contact heterogeneity, and an effective biting rate jointly regulated by temperature and endogenous feedback.
Models were calibrated using data from Guangdong during 2016–2019. Temperature-driven models captured epidemic seasonality but retained systematic peak bias (R² = 0.7394 and 0.7687), whereas incorporating endogenous feedback substantially improved multi-year epidemic reconstruction (R² = 0.8883 and 0.8949). Both feedback pathways showed similar state dependent patterns, facilitating transmission-related processes at lower infection burdens but suppressing or limiting further amplification at higher burdens. 
Symbolic regression converted these nonlinear relationships into explicit mathematical functions. Cross-temporal and regional analyses supported broadly consistent functional structures, although feedback strength varied among regions. 
These findings suggest that endogenous feedback may regulate realised human–mosquito contact and, together with external drivers, shape interannual dengue transmission dynamics.
